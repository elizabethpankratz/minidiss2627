Lit review

What’s the point of a lit review?

  • To give your readers the necessary context for your research.
  • To motivate why your research question is interesting.
  • To explain why you make the predictions that you make.

How do we know what’s important to say about a given paper?

  • Try the following three-step process (hat tip to Dr. Joléne van der Mescht):
    1. What did the authors do? (their question and methods)
    2. What did they find? (their results, prioritising what’s relevant for your RQ)
    3. Your critical comment / evaluation / “so what?”, for example:
      • What implications do their findings have?
      • How do their findings relate to other papers’ findings?
      • What predictions would you make for your RQ, based on their results?

How do we know what order to put ideas in?

  • A good general strategy:
    1. Identify the key message(s) of each paper you want to mention.
    2. Write down all the pieces of information that are needed for understanding the key messages (all keywords, main topics, etc.)
    3. Group together the pieces of information that show up repeatedly in different papers.
    4. Think: Which of those pieces of information are needed for understanding other pieces of information? Which pieces of information can stand alone?
    5. Try to put all the pieces of information in an order that builds the reader’s understanding. Use each piece of information as a stepping stone toward the next piece of information.
  • If the research question combines a couple different ideas (let’s call the ideas A and B), then a good three-part structure is often:
    1. What do we know about A? (Prioritise the detail that’s relevant for the RQ)
      • e.g., statistical learning in general
      • relevant detail: transitional probabilities (TPs) can be different
    2. What do we know about B? (Prioritise the detail that’s relevant for the RQ)
      • e.g., autism in general
      • relevant detail: local processing has priority over global processing
    3. What do we know, and what do we wonder, about how A and B come together? Based on the information from Sections 1 and 2, what predictions can we make?
      • e.g., that autistic people might use distributional cues like TPs differently than allistic people do, because autistic people prioritise lower-level local information

How many papers do we need to cite?

  • There’s no set number that means you’ve cited enough papers.
  • The more papers you read and interact with, the stronger and more well-founded your argument will be.

How much literature is enough? How do we know when to stop reading?

  • Double-check that you’ve covered all the widely-cited key papers (e.g., by searching keywords on Google Scholar and looking for papers cited >100 or especially >1000 times).
  • After that: you’ve probably read enough when all the claims that you want to make are justified/supported by the literature.

Research report

How many words should the introduction / methods / results / discussion / conclusion section have?

  • There’s no set number of words for each section.
  • Focus more on saying everything that you want to say, rather than the specific length of each section.
  • If in doubt, I recommend that you start by writing too much, and then you can trim it down later.

Do we need to submit analysis code?

  • Nope!
  • You just need to report/explain your analysis clearly enough that someone else could replicate it fully without seeing your code (like in the DAPR reports).

Can we include figures/plots in the Discussion section?

  • Depends on the goal of the figure/plot!
  • The Discussion section is for interpreting results, not introducing new results.
  • So if your figure is meant to help the reader interpret your findings, then yes absolutely.

Can the minidiss have a different title than the lit review / poster?

  • Yes, please!
  • Customise your title to the research story you’re trying to tell.
  • I like titles that are a full (but short) sentence that declare the research finding, e.g., “Autistic traits are associated with [X]”.

Do words in figures, tables, and their captions count toward the word count total?

  • Nope. You can include as many words there as you like.

Do words in the abstract count toward the word count total?

  • Nope. The abstract should be max 150 words, and the report should be max 2000 words, so between the two, you have max 2150 words.
